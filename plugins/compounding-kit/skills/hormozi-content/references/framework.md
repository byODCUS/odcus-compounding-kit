# Hormozi Framework — Teach What's Possible and Why

## Scope (redefined by the user, 2026-10-28)

The rule below is a **presentation framework**: Hormozi's one-to-many selling on stage, in webinars and in pitches. It applies where time is limited and the audience decides in the room.

| Format | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Time-boxed formats:** presentations, keynotes, webinars, pitches, sales calls, offers, sales copy on service pages | What's possible and why. No step-by-step. The how is on request. Everything below applies unchanged. |
| **Content:** blog, LinkedIn, newsletter, whitepaper, video, toolbox | **Show the how.** Why first, then the how, with a real example we did ourselves. Give away the method, sell the implementation in the client's own systems. |

the user's reasoning (28.10.2026): with AI especially, people need to see that we can actually do the things. Showing the how is the credibility that lands clients. In a presentation it bores; in content it proves.

**What "show the how" means in content (all must hold):**
1. The why comes first, the how follows. The how never replaces the why.
2. The how is real: we did it ourselves, or it is a tool from the toolbox that was tested. No generic listicle, no steps we never ran.
3. The reader can do it in their own tool this week, alone. Steps that need company systems, other people or ongoing upkeep are named as such, not hidden: that part is the implementation we sell.
4. Concrete enough to copy: actual wording, actual prompt, actual order, actual time it took.
5. Dated: tool names and screens carry `stand` and `pruefen-am`, because the how ages faster than the why.

**Our own setup in content (the user, 30.10.2026): show it, but high level.**
- Allowed: the building blocks and how they connect, in plain roles ("a folder of context the assistant reads first", "a daily check compares what should have run with what did run", "drafts wait in a folder until someone approves them by mail"), the order of a flow, our rules (what never goes out alone), real incidents with dates, what it costs us in time.
- Not allowed: job names, tool, skill or agent names of our internals, schedule times, file paths, hosts and infrastructure, credentials, our internal prompts word for word, repo details.
- Test: could a reader rebuild the idea in their own tools? Good. Could an attacker map our machine room from it? Then it is too deep.

## Core principle (time-boxed formats)

Most content that tries to be "valuable" teaches execution — step-by-step how-to. This fails because:

1. Some readers find a detail that doesn't apply to their situation
2. Others feel overwhelmed
3. Others feel like they can do it themselves

None of those three outcomes includes buying from you, hiring you, or trusting you more.

The alternative: teach what's possible and why it works.

---

## The wrong way (execution-focused)

> "So here's what we're gonna do... Open Facebook Ads Manager → create a campaign → set daily budget to $50 → use this type of targeting → write this hook → use this CTA"

Problems:
- Only reaches people who run Facebook ads right now
- Only applies to people at that exact stage
- Makes the audience feel like they can do it themselves
- Overwhelms everyone who isn't ready

---

## The right way (perspective-focused)

> "Most ads don't fail from the technical side. Meaning your targeting, campaign type etc. They fail from weak hooks or lack of testing of creative, copy, or landing pages... here's proof of people who changed it to our way before and after..."

Why this works:
- Applies to everyone in the room — now and later
- Shifts how they see the problem
- Doesn't overwhelm with steps
- Positions the speaker as the person who understands the mechanism
- Creates buying mode: now they want someone to handle the how

---

## The three failure modes of execution content

| Failure mode | What happens |
|---|---|
| "This doesn't apply to me" | Prospect has different setup, different stage, different tools |
| "This is overwhelming" | Too many steps, prospect shuts down |
| "I can do this myself" | You've given away the implementation — prospect doesn't need you |

None of these three leads to a purchase.

---

## Why "what and why" feels like less but is more

People hesitate to teach this way because it feels like giving away less.

The truth: step-by-step content only reaches the small fraction of the room ready to act on that exact thing right now. "What's possible and why it works" applies to everyone — now and in future, as their situation changes.

It shifts worldview and beliefs, not just task lists. That's more durable, more valuable, and reaches more people.

In time-boxed formats the how is what they pay for: the presentation or pitch sells the what and why. In content (blog, LinkedIn, newsletter, whitepaper, video, toolbox) the rule flips since 28.10.2026: give away the method with a real example, sell the implementation in the client's own systems (see Scope).

---

## Structure for perspective-focused content

1. **Name the problem specifically** — not "many businesses struggle with X" but the concrete version of the failure
2. **State what's possible** — what does it look like when this is working? What outcome is achievable?
3. **Explain why it works** — the mechanism, not the method. Why does the problem exist? What's the root cause?
4. **Proof** — before/after, client result, example, counter-intuitive finding. Concrete, not asserted.
5. **Soft CTA** — invite them toward the how. Don't deliver it.
